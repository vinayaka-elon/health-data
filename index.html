Health Data Finder

Every dataset reachable from Python for healthcare analytics, what's in it, how fine-grained it is, when and where it was collected, what it takes to get access, and the code to load it. Pick a topic, then narrow by the models the data supports, by source, or by access tier.

How to use this page, read this before you pick a project

1. Write the question first. One sentence, naming what you are predicting and from what. "Does the share of agency staffing predict a nursing home's inspection score?" Do not browse for a dataset and then invent a question to fit it, that is how projects stall in week 9.

2. Pick one topic. One chip only. The list collapses to the handful of sources that could answer a question in that area.

3. Filter Access to Open first. Open and Synthetic need no account, no form and no waiting. Registration means a free Kaggle or data.gov login. Credentialed and Application mean weeks of paperwork, viable only if you start in week 1.

4. Filter Models to what you intend to fit. Regression needs a continuous outcome, Classification a yes/no one, Deep learning images or signals, LLM text. Every entry lists both outcomes it supports, so you can check the data carries the thing you want to predict.

5. Open the entry and read in this order: the amber warning first, it is where the project dies if you ignore it, then Unit, which tells you what one row is, then Variables to confirm your outcome exists, then How to get it and the copy-paste starter.

6. Download in week 2 and keep that copy. Several sources are re-released continuously, so an analysis run in week 12 against a live URL will not match what you wrote in week 3. Save the file, record the date, and cite your archived copy.

7. Two sources at most. If you join, the entry names the key, usually CCN, NPI, county FIPS, SEQN or a state code. Read ID columns as str, or a leading zero disappears and the join silently returns nothing. Always print the row count before and after:

df = a.merge(b, on="Facility ID", how="inner")
print(len(a), len(b), "->", len(df))   # if this is 0, check your dtypes

Before you commit to a source, confirm three things: you can download it today without asking anyone; one row is the unit you actually want to model; and the outcome you plan to predict is a real column, not something you hope to derive. If any of the three is shaky, pick again now, while it is still week 1.

Seven things to know before picking one

  1. Access tier is the schedule risk, not the analysis. Credentialed PhysioNet data needs CITI "Data or Specimens Only Research" training plus a signed DUA, budget at least a week, often more. A team that picks MIMIC in week 10 will not finish.
  2. A credentialed DUA forbids sending the data to a third party, which includes commercial LLM APIs and online platforms. If a project involves clinical notes and a language model, the model must run locally, and that constraint is itself worth writing up.
  3. Survey data without weights is wrong, not merely imprecise. NHANES, BRFSS and MEPS all require their design variables. Python's tooling here is thinner than R's, samplics is the closest equivalent to R's survey.
  4. Spontaneous-report databases have no denominator. openFDA's FAERS and MAUDE count reports, not events. They cannot be read as incidence or compared across products without exposure data.
  5. Modeled estimates are not measurements. CDC PLACES values come from a model fitted to BRFSS, so a tract's "prevalence" is a prediction. Treat it as a covariate, not as surveillance.
  6. Unversioned sources break reproducibility. ClinVar, COSMIC, TCGA and the openFDA endpoints are re-released continuously. Have every team download and checksum their extract in week 2 and cite that archived copy, not the live URL.
  7. Toy resolution gives toy conclusions. MedMNIST is 28×28 by default. It is excellent for learning a pipeline and useless for any claim about clinical performance, use the 224 px MedMNIST+ variants if the project makes one.

The AI benchmark entries note saturation and contamination status, which is the fastest-moving field here, re-check scores before quoting them. Final project

You will answer one question with data, end to end: find it, clean it, describe it, test it, model it, and say honestly what it does and does not show. Groups of three. The question is yours; the standard is not.

The one-sentence test. Before anything else, write your project as a single sentence naming what you are predicting and what you are predicting it from. "Does the share of agency staffing predict a nursing home's inspection score?" If you cannot write that sentence, you do not have a project yet, you have a topic.

What you hand in

  1. A notebook that runs. Restart the kernel, run all, no errors, top to bottom. If it does not run, it is not graded.
  2. Your archived data file, with the date you downloaded it. Not a link.
  3. A four-page write-up: question, data and cleaning, methods, results, limitations. Every number in it appears in a notebook output.
  4. Ten minutes of slides. One question, one chart that carries the finding, one honest limitation.

Methods you must use

RequiredWhat counts
Descriptive statisticsCentre, spread and shape for your outcome and main predictors. If a distribution is skewed, say so and say what you did.
VisualizationFour charts minimum, each answering a question. A correlation heatmap is one of them. Every axis labelled with units.
Hypothesis testingTwo tests, with H₀ and H₁ written out, the test chosen and justified, and a conclusion a non-statistician could read.
RegressionSimple regression with diagnostics, then a full model. Interpret coefficients in real units. Report R² for both and say whether the extra predictors earned their place.
Logistic regression or a classifierOnly if your data has a yes/no outcome. Confusion matrix and the class balance are both required, accuracy alone is not a result.
Machine learningOptional. A train/test split and one model beyond regression. Report test performance, never training performance alone.

How projects fail

Every one of these has happened. None is hypothetical.

  • Picking credentialed data in week 9. The paperwork takes longer than the time left. Check the access badge before you fall in love with a dataset.
  • No outcome variable. "We'll explore trends in X" produces charts and no project. Name the column you are predicting in week 2.
  • Running dropna() without looking. The rows that go missing are often the ones that matter most. Compare the dropped rows against the kept ones before you delete anything.
  • Reporting accuracy on an imbalanced outcome. If 90% of rows are "No", a model that always says "No" scores 90%. Report the baseline next to your accuracy, always.
  • Leaking the answer. If one predictor is what the outcome was calculated from, your model is reading the answer key. Drop it and say why.
  • Claiming causation. You have observational data. "Associated with" is the strongest phrase available to you.
  • Analysing from a live URL. The numbers change under you and the write-up stops matching the notebook.
  • Three sources. Every join loses rows. Two is the ceiling.

Two things worth more than a clean result

A limitation you found yourself. Not the generic ones, the specific one that applies to your data. That the ED sample has no athlete-exposure denominator. That the survey weights change what the estimate means. That your two sources define the same variable differently. These are worth more than another decimal place.

A negative result, reported straight. If your predictor does nothing, say so and say what you would need to detect an effect if one existed. A well-executed null finding earns full marks. A story assembled after the fact does not.

Questions about scope or access, ask before week 3, not after.

Project launch workshop

Sixty minutes. Every group leaves with three things: a one-sentence question, a dataset that loaded on their own laptop, and a named outcome column. Nothing else is the goal, and nothing less is acceptable.

Built for 10 groups of three. Laptops required. Start the clock when you start talking.

0:00 – 0:051. Frame the gate

Open the Project guide tab and read the one-sentence test aloud. Put one good sentence and one bad one on screen side by side.

"Does the share of agency staffing predict a nursing home's inspection score?", that is a project. "We want to look at nursing home quality", that is a topic. By 11 o'clock every group here has the first kind, a dataset open on a laptop, and the name of the column you are predicting. If you leave with anything less, you will be behind for the rest of the term.

State the two hard rules now so nobody argues later: one or two sources, and Open or Registration access only.

0:05 – 0:122. Silent three

No talking. Every student, individually, writes three candidate questions on paper. Each one must name a thing being predicted and a thing predicting it.

Why silent. In a group of three, whoever speaks first sets the agenda and the other two start editing. Seven minutes of silence puts nine ideas on the table. Enforce it, the room will try to start talking at minute two.

Circulate but do not answer questions yet. If someone is stuck, point at one sport, one condition, or one facility type and move on.

0:12 – 0:223. Converge to one

Groups read all nine aloud, then cut on two criteria only:

  1. Can you name the outcome column, or at least say what kind of number it is?
  2. Does data for this plausibly exist and is anyone allowed to have it?

Pick one. Write the sentence on the card. Write the runner-up down too, they will need it in phase 4 more often than they expect.

Watch for: groups picking the most interesting question over the most answerable one. Interesting is cheap. Ask them which one they could still be working on in week 12.
0:22 – 0:374. Data hunt, laptops open

Send them to the Find data tab. One topic chip, Access set to Open, Models set to whatever they intend to fit. Open two or three entries, read the amber warning first, then copy the starter code and run it.

The gate for this phase is a printed output. Not a bookmark, not a plan to download later. They must see df.shape and their outcome column in a cell output before the clock runs out.

Fifteen minutes. If the code has not run by the end of it, you switch to your backup question. That is not failure, that is the hour doing its job. Finding out now costs you fifteen minutes. Finding out in week nine costs you the project.
Watch for: a group three levels deep in a credentialed dataset. Check the access badge over their shoulder and redirect immediately.
0:37 – 0:475. Red team, both directions

Pair the groups, five pairs. Five minutes each direction, and the clock splits it for you.

The reviewing group works down the checklist out loud. The presenting group may not defend itself, they listen and write. That rule is the whole exercise; say it twice.

Red-team checklist, read each one aloud

  1. Say their sentence back to them. If you cannot, they do not have one.
  2. Name their outcome column. Point at it in their output. If it is not on screen, it does not exist.
  3. What is one row? A patient, a hospital, a county, a day? If the group hesitates, flag it.
  4. Check the access badge. Open or Registration? If it is Credentialed or Application, stop and say so now.
  5. How many sources? More than two, or no join key named for the second, flag it.
  6. Does any predictor give away the answer? Is one column the thing the outcome was computed from?
  7. Read their amber warning out loud. Ask what they are going to do about it. "Nothing" is a real answer only if they can say why.
  8. What would a boring result look like? If they cannot describe one, they are planning to find what they already believe.
0:47 – 0:556. Revise while I circulate

Groups fix what the red team found. You go table to table, 45 seconds each, which is exactly enough for ten groups with slack.

Ask one question at every table and then move:

Show me the output where your outcome column appears.

Everything else can wait for office hours. If the answer is "we are about to", the group is behind and you should say so before you walk away.

0:55 – 1:007. Commit out loud

Each group stands and gives twenty seconds: the sentence, the dataset name, and the number of rows. No slides, no preamble, no apologising. Ten groups fits in four minutes if you hold the line.

Collect the cards on the way out.

Why out loud. A sentence said in front of nine other groups is harder to quietly abandon next week than one written on a form. This is the point of the last five minutes, do not let it get cut when the hour runs long.

Before the room walks in

  • Send this page the night before, pointed at the Project guide tab. Ask them to arrive with one question already in mind, the silent phase goes better when it is not cold.
  • Check that pandas loads on the room's wifi. Half of phase 4 dies on a proxy, not on the data.
  • Print the project card. Paper is faster than a form and you can read ten of them in the lift.
  • Have three fallback datasets ready for groups who stall: SUPPORT2, CDC PLACES county, NEISS. All three are one file, open, and load in a single line.

What success looks like at 1:00

Ten cards in your hand, each with a sentence you could repeat back, a dataset name, a row count and a column name. Perhaps two groups will have switched to their backup question, and that is the hour working as intended. What you should not have is a group still deciding between three topics, if you see that at 0:47, give them your pick and move on. They can change it later; they cannot get the week back.
